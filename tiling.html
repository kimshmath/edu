The Infinite Floor

How shapes conspire to fill the plane — and what happens when they refuse to repeat

Section I

The Alhambra's Secret

In the south of Spain, perched on a hill overlooking Granada, there stands a palace that has been teaching mathematics for seven hundred years. The Alhambra was built by the Nasrid dynasty in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries — the last great flowering of Moorish civilization on the Iberian peninsula. Its walls are covered in carved stucco, glazed ceramic, and painted plaster, and within these decorations hides an extraordinary achievement: the Moorish artists discovered, by hand and eye alone, essentially every way that a motif can be repeated across a flat surface.

They did not have group theory. They did not have the language of symmetry operations — rotations, reflections, glide reflections, translations. What they had was an aesthetic imperative rooted in Islamic theology: the avoidance of figural representation, and a consequent devotion to geometric pattern. This constraint became a liberation. Freed from the task of depicting the world, the artisans of the Alhambra turned their attention to the structure of repetition itself.

A repeating pattern on a plane — what mathematicians call a wallpaper pattern — seems at first to offer infinite variety. You might tile a floor with squares, or with hexagons, or with interlocking fish à la Escher. But the symmetries of these patterns are not infinitely varied. In 1891, the Russian crystallographer Evgraf Fedorov proved a theorem that the Moorish artists had intuited half a millennium earlier: there are exactly seventeen distinct symmetry types for patterns that repeat in two directions. Not sixteen, not eighteen. Seventeen.

Seventeen — that is the number of ways the plane can remember a motif and repeat it forever, each way a different dialect of the same language of symmetry.

The number seventeen is not arbitrary. It falls out of a careful algebraic argument about which combinations of rotational symmetries are compatible with a repeating lattice. A wallpaper pattern can contain rotations of order two, three, four, or six — but never five, never seven. This is the crystallographic restriction, and it governs everything from Arabic tilework to the atomic lattices of crystals. The proof is surprisingly geometric: if a lattice has a rotation of order n, the rotated copies of any lattice point must themselves form a lattice. For five-fold or seven-fold rotations, this leads to contradictions — points forced closer and closer together, violating the discreteness of the lattice.

The seventeen groups were independently rediscovered by the Hungarian mathematician George Pólya in 1924, who was unaware of Fedorov's earlier work. A few years later, Pólya's paper fell into the hands of a Dutch graphic artist named M.C. Escher, who had himself been obsessed with the Alhambra since a visit in 1922. Escher did not understand the mathematics — he later wrote that the abstract notation gave him "vertigo" — but he grasped the underlying idea with an artist's intuition. He spent the next four decades filling notebooks with tessellations, exploring every one of the seventeen groups, bending the rigid tiles into lizards, angels, horsemen, and demons.

"I often seem to have more in common with mathematicians than with my fellow artists."

— M.C. Escher, 1958

What exactly are these seventeen symmetry types? Each wallpaper group is a recipe: it specifies which symmetry operations — rotations, reflections, and glide reflections — combine with the two translation directions to generate the full pattern. The simplest group, called p1, has only translations: no rotations, no reflections. The pattern repeats, but it never flips or turns. At the other extreme, p6m packs in six-fold rotations, mirror lines in six directions, and glide reflections — the maximum symmetry a wallpaper pattern can possess. Between these extremes lie fifteen more possibilities, each a unique combination of symmetry elements.

The beauty of this classification is not merely taxonomic. It reveals a deep truth about the nature of two-dimensional space: the plane itself has a limited vocabulary for repetition. No matter how inventive the designer, no matter how complex the motif, the underlying symmetry must be one of these seventeen types. The Moorish artists discovered this ceiling empirically; Fedorov and Pólya proved it was a floor as well. There is nothing above and nothing below. Seventeen is the whole story.

Section II

The Geometer's Jigsaw

Seventeen symmetries tell us how a pattern can repeat, but they do not tell us which shapes will do the repeating. A tiling is more than a symmetry; it is an act of fitting. Specific polygons, laid edge-to-edge without gaps or overlaps, must cover the plane. If the tiles are regular polygons — equilateral triangles, squares, hexagons — the story is short and ancient. Only these three, among all the regular shapes, can tile the plane by themselves. A regular pentagon, with its five-fold rotational symmetry, cannot: its interior angle of 108° does not divide 360° evenly, and three pentagons meeting at a vertex leave a gap of 36°, a narrow wedge the shape of a golden gnomon.

But the world of convex polygons is not exhausted by regular ones. Triangles tile the plane in every imaginable way — any triangle tiles, since two copies joined along an edge form a parallelogram, and parallelograms tile trivially. Quadrilaterals tile as well, even when they are not convex. The interesting question begins at five sides: which convex pentagons can tile the plane?

This question, deceptively simple, has a long and surprising history. In 1918, the German mathematician Karl Reinhardt, a student of Ludwig Bieberbach, identified five types of convex pentagons that tile the plane — five infinite families, each defined by constraints on angles and edges. Reinhardt believed his list was complete. He was wrong. In 1968, Richard Kershner added three more types and again declared the problem solved. He, too, was wrong.

The question of which pentagons tile the plane attracted not only professional geometers, but also a San Diego homemaker with no formal training — and she would discover more new pentagons than anyone before her.

In July 1975, Martin Gardner wrote a Scientific American column about Kershner's eight types. Among his readers was Marjorie Rice, a housewife with a high-school education and a fierce interest in puzzles. She devised her own notation — symbols and diagrams of her own invention, which professional mathematicians struggled to read — and within two years had discovered four new pentagon types that no one had found before. Her work was verified by Doris Schattschneider, who helped translate Rice's idiosyncratic notation into standard mathematical language.

"I began to feel that all my life had been leading up to this point. I could not have explained why; it was a feeling I had."

— Marjorie Rice, reflecting on her pentagon discoveries

The list grew. A fourteenth type was found by Rolf Stein in 1985. The problem then sat, apparently solved, for thirty years. In 2015, a team at the University of Washington Bothell — Casey Mann, Jennifer McLoud-Mann, and David Von Derau — used a computer search to find a fifteenth type: a pentagon with very specific angles and edges, one that had eluded both human searchers and earlier algorithms. It was announced with some fanfare, and many mathematicians assumed this, at last, must be the end.

Two years later, it was. In 2017, the French mathematician Michaël Rao proved by an exhaustive computer-assisted search that exactly fifteen types of convex pentagons tile the plane. No sixteenth type exists. The problem that Reinhardt had opened in 1918, and that a housewife in San Diego had quietly advanced from her kitchen table, was closed ninety-nine years later by a rigorous enumeration over all possible angle and edge configurations.

What lies in this list is strange. Some pentagons are symmetric, with reflective axes and neat ratios. Others are almost freakish — irregular shapes with seemingly arbitrary constraints, angles of 60° and 135° and 105°, edges in ratios that satisfy bizarre equations. Each one is an answer to the question how can a pentagon fit with copies of itself? Each is, in its way, a small miracle: a fragment of the plane that recognizes itself in rotation, reflection, or translation, and locks together without a seam.

Section III

Breaking the Crystal

Until the 1960s, mathematicians believed that every set of shapes which tiles the plane must do so periodically — that is, by repeating some finite configuration along two independent translation vectors. This belief had the status of folklore. It was widely assumed, carefully never stated as a theorem, and it turned out to be spectacularly false. The story of how it fell apart begins with a question about decidability.

In 1961, the logician Hao Wang considered a seemingly innocuous problem. Suppose you are given a finite set of square tiles, each edge colored in one of a finite number of colors, and a rule that two tiles may only be placed next to each other if their shared edges match in color. Can you decide, algorithmically, whether any given set of such Wang tiles can tile the plane? Wang conjectured that the answer was yes — and furthermore, that if a set can tile the plane at all, it must be able to do so periodically. If his conjecture held, the decision problem would reduce to a finite search.

In 1966, Wang's student Robert Berger proved the conjecture false, and he did so by finding an explicit aperiodic set. Berger constructed 20,426 Wang tiles that could tile the plane, but only in non-periodic ways. The proof was a tour de force of combinatorial ingenuity. It also established that the tiling problem is algorithmically undecidable — there is no procedure that can examine an arbitrary set of Wang tiles and reliably determine whether they tile the plane at all.

An aperiodic tile set tiles the plane, but never periodically. It is a finite set of shapes whose only allowed global arrangements are infinite and non-repeating — patterns that refuse to fold back on themselves.

Berger's set of 20,426 tiles was dramatic but impractical. Over the next decade, mathematicians raced to reduce the number. Berger himself brought it down to 104. In 1971, Raphael Robinson reduced it to 6 square tiles with matching rules enforced by notches. Then in 1973, Roger Penrose discovered something astonishing: just two tiles — a "kite" and a "dart," or equivalently a thick rhombus and a thin rhombus — suffice, provided they are marked to prevent periodic assembly. The Penrose tilings possess five-fold symmetry, which we saw in Section I is forbidden for periodic patterns. This is no coincidence: any tiling with five-fold symmetry must be aperiodic.

"It is a matter of curious experience to find a constructible pattern in two-dimensional space which has five-fold symmetry. Such a thing seems at first impossible, for the plane cannot be tiled by regular pentagons."

— Roger Penrose, 1974

What makes Penrose tilings most remarkable is not just that they are aperiodic — it is that they are self-similar under a scaling operation called inflation. Given any Penrose tiling, one can divide each kite and each dart into smaller kites and darts using a specific rule. The resulting tiling is still a Penrose tiling, but with smaller tiles. Conversely, one can group tiles together (deflation) to produce a larger-scale Penrose tiling. This means that any finite patch of a Penrose tiling appears, infinitely often, at every scale — zoom in anywhere, and you find the same local configurations repeated, yet the overall pattern never exactly repeats.

The density of kites to darts in a Penrose tiling is precisely the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618 — an irrational number. This irrationality is not a coincidence either; it is the fingerprint of aperiodicity. A periodic tiling, with its fundamental domain, can only have rational densities. Penrose tilings, born of five-fold symmetry, carry φ in their bones.

A further curiosity: any two Penrose tilings of the plane, chosen from the uncountably many distinct ones, are locally indistinguishable. That is, any finite patch you can find in one tiling, you can find in every other. A Penrose tiling has no "local" identity — its global structure is determined by asymptotic information that no finite observer can access. It is as if the entire tiling exists only as a coherent whole, each finite patch a fragment that belongs simultaneously to every possible completion.

IV

Forbidden Symmetry

Quasicrystals and the impossible pattern

Nineteenth-century crystallographers had proved a theorem so fundamental it entered the textbooks as a law of nature. The crystallographic restriction theorem stated that a periodic arrangement of atoms in the plane — the basis of every crystal known to science — could only possess rotational symmetry of order two, three, four, or six. No other rotations would do. The argument was elementary and seemed airtight: if a lattice were invariant under a rotation of 72°, for instance, you could apply the rotation to any two points of the lattice and obtain a new lattice point closer than the original spacing, contradicting the assumption that the lattice had a minimum distance between points. Five-fold symmetry was forbidden. So were seven, eight, nine, ten, and every order greater than six except six itself.

This theorem governed the science of crystals for a century. The fourteen Bravais lattices, the 230 space groups, every description of every known crystal structure — all obeyed the restriction without exception. To suggest otherwise was to misunderstand what a crystal was.

And then, on the morning of April 8, 1982, at the National Bureau of Standards in Gaithersburg, Maryland, an Israeli materials scientist named Dan Shechtman fired a beam of electrons through a rapidly cooled alloy of aluminum and manganese. He was looking at the diffraction pattern. What he saw was ten bright spots arranged in a perfect ring around the center, and beyond that another ring, and another — each with ten-fold rotational symmetry. In his laboratory notebook, next to sample 1725, he wrote three letters: 10 Fold ???

"There is no such animal."

— Shechtman's colleagues, on first hearing of his discovery

Ten-fold diffraction symmetry implied five-fold atomic symmetry. Five-fold atomic symmetry implied a crystal that violated the crystallographic restriction. It implied, in short, something that could not exist. Shechtman checked his instrument. He checked his sample. He checked the diffraction pattern from every angle and along every direction. The pattern was real. The crystal, however, was impossible.

His colleagues did not believe him. The head of his research group asked him to re-read a textbook on crystallography. He was eventually asked to leave the group. For two years his paper sat unpublished, rejected by journals that could not bring themselves to print a finding that contradicted a century of established science. When the paper finally appeared in Physical Review Letters in November 1984, it drew immediate attack from one of the most eminent chemists of the century.

"There are no quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists."

— Linus Pauling, double Nobel laureate, on Shechtman's findings

But Shechtman's pattern was what it was. Within weeks of his publication, the Princeton physicist Paul Steinhardt and his student Dov Levine proposed an explanation. The atoms in Shechtman's alloy, they argued, were not arranged periodically. They were arranged quasiperiodically — in a pattern that never quite repeated, but whose local structure recurred at every scale, with the golden ratio controlling the distances. It was the three-dimensional analogue of a Penrose tiling. Steinhardt coined a name for it: quasicrystal.

The mathematics had been waiting for the physics. Alan Mackay, a crystallographer at Birkbeck College, had already calculated in 1982 — the same year as Shechtman's discovery, and independently of it — that a Penrose tiling would produce a diffraction pattern with sharp peaks in ten-fold symmetry. When Steinhardt saw Shechtman's electron micrograph, he recognized it immediately. The impossible pattern was exactly the one Mackay had predicted.

The crystallographic restriction had not been wrong. It had been incomplete. The theorem applied to periodic lattices, and it remains true for every periodic lattice. What Shechtman had found was a material that was neither periodic nor amorphous — a third state of ordered matter that the theorem simply did not describe. In 1992 the International Union of Crystallography quietly revised the definition of a crystal. The new definition made no mention of periodicity. A crystal was now simply a solid with a sharp, discrete diffraction pattern — periodic or otherwise.

"If there is one particular lesson we are taking from Shechtman's research, it is not to underestimate the imagination of nature herself."

In 2011, Dan Shechtman received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, alone. By then, hundreds of quasicrystalline materials had been discovered. They have been found in Swedish steel, in engine coatings, in meteorites from the earliest moments of the solar system. The physics had followed the mathematics, and the mathematics had followed the tiling, and the tiling had been there all along — a fragment of the plane that refused to repeat, a symmetry that had been forbidden and was not.

V

The One Stone

David Smith's hat and the end of a sixty-year search

Penrose had found a pair of tiles. But the question, obvious almost as soon as he posed it, went unanswered for another fifty years: could a single tile do it? A single shape that tiles the plane and only aperiodically — no periodic tiling possible, no matching rules, no decorations, no decorative arrows on its edges, no asterisks in the fine print. Just a shape. This was the einstein problem, named not for Albert but for the German ein Stein, one stone. It was the holy grail of combinatorial tiling theory.

Many mathematicians doubted such a shape could exist. The closest approach, the Socolar–Taylor tile of 2010, required matching rules that constrained non-adjacent tiles — not quite a shape standing alone. Other partial solutions stretched the definition of a tile until a tile became disconnected pieces, or until the rules governing it crept back in. For decades the grail stayed just out of reach.

In November 2022, an email arrived in the inbox of Craig Kaplan, a computer scientist at the University of Waterloo who studies tilings. The sender was a retired print technician from Yorkshire named David Smith, a self-described "shape hobbyist" with no formal mathematical training. Attached was a 13-sided polygon that Smith had been cutting out of cardstock at his kitchen table. He had noticed something odd: the shape could fit together with copies of itself, and also with copies of its mirror image, but he could not make it repeat.

"I'm always messing about and experimenting with shapes. Although it's certainly nice to be the first to discover something strange, it's just part of the way I enjoy myself."

— David Smith, describing his amateur practice

Kaplan looked at the shape, which Smith had been calling "the hat" — because, turned one way, it resembled a fedora; turned the other way, a T-shirt. Within a few weeks of computational experimentation, Kaplan was convinced. The hat was the einstein. The proof, however, required expertise beyond his own. Kaplan and Smith contacted Chaim Goodman-Strauss, a longtime tiling theorist, who brought in the software developer Joseph Samuel Myers. Within days, Myers — working at extraordinary speed — produced the first of two independent aperiodicity proofs.

The shape itself is almost mundane. It is a polykite: the union of eight kites cut from the [3.4.6.4] Laves tiling, a familiar decoration of the plane by identical kite-shaped tiles, each with angles 60°, 90°, 120°, 90°. Eight of these kites, glued along matching edges in a particular arrangement, produce a 13-sided polygon. It is the kind of shape a child might draw. That such a simple figure should possess the property no one had managed to exhibit in sixty years was, as Goodman-Strauss put it, "a once-in-a-lifetime thing."

On March 20, 2023, Smith, Myers, Kaplan, and Goodman-Strauss posted their preprint on arXiv. The paper went viral. Within days it had been reported in the New York Times, Scientific American, and Nature. The mathematical community received the result with a kind of delighted disbelief. A proof had been assembled by an amateur, a software engineer, and two mathematicians in a matter of months, resolving a problem that had resisted attack since the 1960s.

There was, however, a subtlety. Every tiling by hats uses a mixture of the hat and its mirror image. The hat is not chiral — tiling requires reflections, not just rotations and translations. Some purists argued this violated the strictest reading of the einstein problem. Within two months, Smith and his collaborators had answered them. In May 2023, they announced a second shape — a close relative of the hat, called the Spectre — that tiles the plane aperiodically using only translations and rotations, with no reflections allowed. The grail was thoroughly won.

"The hat was hiding in plain sight. Mathematicians had been looking for it for sixty years. An amateur found it by cutting out shapes and noticing something strange."

The cultural response exceeded anything Shechtman or Penrose had known. Within weeks the hat had been knitted into scarves, printed on T-shirts, cut from stained glass, rendered in origami. The United Kingdom Mathematics Trust and the National Museum of Mathematics launched the Einstein Mad Hat Awards, which drew 245 submissions from 32 countries. A tiling problem from pure geometry had, briefly, become a matter of popular culture — an object people wanted to own. And at the center of it stood David Smith, a hobbyist from Bridlington who had solved, in his spare time, a problem the best minds in combinatorics had not.

What closes, in 2023, is not simply the einstein problem. It is a chapter of mathematical history that runs from Berger's 20,426 tiles in 1966, through Robinson's six and Penrose's two, down to the single hat. A sixty-year arc of reduction, ending in a shape so simple a child could cut it out. Every tiling by hats is aperiodic. No period, no repetition, no translational symmetry — just the shape, forever rearranging itself, never once locking into a pattern.

Coda

The story that begins in fourteenth-century Granada and ends on David Smith's kitchen table is, in one sense, a story about surfaces: about what can cover a plane and what cannot, about which symmetries close up into repetition and which forbid it. But the deeper narrative is about the relationship between constraint and freedom. A few simple local rules — edges that must match, angles that must fit, tiles that must adjoin — generate patterns of unbounded complexity, patterns that contain every finite configuration infinitely many times yet never once repeat themselves globally. Among the mathematicians who glimpsed this most clearly was Roger Penrose himself, who saw in his own tilings a miniature of something far larger.

"My interest in the tiles has to do with the idea of a universe controlled by very simple forces, even though we see complications all over the place. The tilings follow conventional rules to make complicated patterns. It was an attempt to see how the complicated could be satisfied by very simple rules that reflect what we see in the world."

— Roger Penrose, interview in Discover Magazine, 2009

Penrose's remark reaches past tilings toward physics, toward the puzzle of why such a tidy handful of fundamental equations — Einstein's, Schrödinger's, Dirac's — should suffice to specify a universe as intricate as ours. The aperiodic tilings are a small-scale laboratory for the question. They show, in geometry so simple that Kepler and Dürer could draw its beginnings, that determinism and novelty are not opposites. A law that fixes what can sit beside what does not thereby fix the whole; it leaves room, infinite room, for patterns that no one has ever seen and that will never recur. The Alhambra's tile-makers knew this in their craft. Penrose knew it in his kites and darts. Smith rediscovered it in his kitchen, cutting shapes from cardstock, one at a time, until the hat emerged.

What the chapter traces, then, is less a sequence of theorems than a slowly deepening understanding that the plane — the simplest two-dimensional space there is — holds more than anyone had imagined. Seventeen wallpaper groups, fifteen types of convex pentagon, two Penrose tiles, one hat. The grail was a single stone. It turned out to be hiding in plain sight, and to have been waiting there the whole time.

"They are just nice to look at."
— PENROSE, ON WHY HE CHOSE PENTAGONS
