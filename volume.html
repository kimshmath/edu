The Slicer of Syracuse

시라쿠사의 분할자

Geometry began in the dirt. For the ancient thinkers who etched their first lines into the Mediterranean sand, straight edges were honest. A square could be cleanly fractured into triangles; a rectangle could be measured with rigid certainty. But a curve held a deeper, more elusive secret.

The circle, flawlessly smooth and lacking any sharp corners to grasp, slipped away from their measuring tools. It bends endlessly, evading the logic of early mathematics. How does one precisely measure the area of a shape that never stays straight long enough to be pinned down?

It was Archimedes of Syracuse who dared to lay a trap for infinity. Recognizing that he could not measure the circle directly, he decided to cage it. By placing one perfect polygon completely inside the circle and tightly wrapping another around the outside, he created an inescapable mathematical vice. The true, unknowable area of the perfect curve was forever condemned to live in the narrow shadow between the two jagged boundaries.

"I am persuaded that certain theorems not yet demonstrated will become evident to future generations."

As he systematically multiplied the number of edges—from hexagons to dodecagons, all the way up to ninety-six sides—the straight lines began to mirror the curve, carving away the empty space. This grueling method of exhaustion did not merely approximate; Archimedes logically proved that the gap could be forced smaller than any assignable grain of sand.

In doing so, he brushed against the concept of a limit, capturing the ghost of infinity two millennia before the formal invention of calculus.

Ghosts of Departed Quantities

사라진 양들의 유령

After antiquity, geometry slept. When it finally awoke during the Enlightenment, it had fundamentally transformed. Mathematics was no longer obsessed with static objects frozen in place; it had been infected by the concept of motion. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz realized that a curve was not just a bent line—it was the physical trajectory of a moving point through time.

To calculate the area accumulating beneath this moving point, they required a dangerous conceptual leap. Instead of cutting a shape into a few simple triangles, they imagined slicing the area into an infinite number of infinitely thin vertical strips. These strips had practically zero width, yet somehow possessed a measurable height.

“And what are these fluxions? The velocities of evanescent increments? They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?” — George Berkeley, The Analyst (1734)

This paradox sparked philosophical outrage across Europe. The rigorous critics of the era, most notably Bishop George Berkeley, famously mocked these infinitely small slices as illogical. How could a width be smaller than any possible measurement, yet greater than nothing at all? The calculus of the 17th century was practically mathematical witchcraft. It produced miraculous results, but the logical foundation was built on sand.

It took over a century for the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann to exorcise these ghosts. Riemann abandoned the murky philosophy of the infinitely small. Instead, he built a rigid, unyielding scaffolding. He chopped the domain into a finite, countable grid of ordinary rectangles with real, tangible widths.

By taking the strict mathematical limit as the grid became infinitely dense, the jagged staircases of these rectangles forcefully melted into the exact, undeniable area beneath the curve. The witchcraft was finally tamed into pure geometry.

The Merchant’s Coins

상인의 동전

As the 19th century drew to a close, mathematicians began to discover monsters. They conceptualized "pathological" spaces—curves that were infinitely jagged, scattered clouds of disconnected points, and shapes so fractured that they possessed no continuous lines at all. The elegant machinery of calculus was suddenly faced with chaos.

If you attempt to cage this mathematical dust using Riemann’s vertical slices, the method violently collapses. Because the heights of these fractured functions jump wildly from one point to the exact next, the height of any vertical rectangle fluctuates unpredictably depending on exactly where the slice falls. The calculated area refuses to settle. The limit shatters.

« Je suppose que j'aie à payer une somme; je pourrai retirer de mon porte-monnaie les pièces les unes après les autres... C'est l'intégrale de Riemann. Mais je pourrai aussi opérer autrement. Je réunirai toutes les pièces de même valeur... C'est mon intégrale. » — Henri Lebesgue (1926)

In 1902, a young French mathematician named Henri Lebesgue rescued geometry by simply turning his head sideways. Instead of slicing the space vertically along the floor, he sliced it horizontally along the wall. He stopped asking where the point was, and started asking how high the point was.

Lebesgue grouped all points of the exact same height together into a single conceptual bucket, regardless of how wildly scattered they were across the space. He then measured the total collective width of that bucket, and multiplied it by the height. By sorting the infinite chaos by value rather than location, Lebesgue transformed unmeasurable dust into perfectly weighed horizontal bands.

The Shattered Sphere

조각난 구

Lebesgue had seemingly perfected the art of measuring. He proved that almost any shape, no matter how chaotic or fractured, could be logically sorted and assigned a precise volume. But the word almost hid a fatal trapdoor. In 1905, the Italian mathematician Giuseppe Vitali opened it, and geometry fell through.

Vitali asked a dangerous question: could there exist a set of points so bizarre, so fundamentally broken, that even Lebesgue’s mighty horizontal sorting would fail to measure it? Using the controversial Axiom of Choice, Vitali fractured the real number line into infinitely many shifted copies of a single unmeasurable ghost set. If you tried to assign it a length of zero, the total math collapsed to zero. If you assigned it any length greater than zero, the total exploded to infinity.

This terrifying discovery revealed that length and volume are not inherent physical properties of space. They are human constructs, and they can be broken. This realization culminated in the most infamous theorem in modern geometry: the Banach-Tarski Paradox.

"Sur la décomposition des ensembles de points en parties respectivement congruentes." — Stefan Banach & Alfred Tarski, Fundamenta Mathematicae (1924)

In 1924, Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved that it is mathematically possible to take a solid, 3D sphere, shatter it into just five jagged, unmeasurable pieces, and reassemble those exact same pieces just by translating and rotating them to form two solid spheres, each identical to the original.

Because the five pieces are "non-measurable," they possess no defined volume. While suspended in this unmeasurable state, the normal laws of physical conservation cease to exist. (For a deeper exploration of this phenomenon, visit edu.kimsh.kr/banach-tarski.html).

The Weight of Tomorrow

내일의 무게

If the volume of space could be fundamentally broken, how could measurement survive? The answer came not from physics, but from a seemingly unrelated corner of mathematics. In 1933, the Russian mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov provided the ultimate abstraction, rescuing the concept of measure forever.

For centuries, probability had been treated as a gambler’s parlor trick—a loose set of rules for analyzing dice and cards. But Kolmogorov realized that the mathematical machinery used to calculate "area" and "volume" did not need a physical shape to exist. He mapped the entire geometry of space directly onto the geometry of chance.

« Теория вероятностей, как математическая дисциплина, может и должна быть аксиоматизирована совершенно в том же смысле, как геометрия или алгебра. »
(The theory of probability, as a mathematical discipline, can and should be axiomatized in exactly the same sense as geometry and algebra.) — Andrey Kolmogorov, Foundations of the Theory of Probability (1933)

Kolmogorov established that if you define the entire universe of all possible futures as a space with a total "volume" of exactly one, then the "size" of any single event is simply its probability of occurring. Uncertainty itself possessed a rigorous geometry.

Suddenly, the methods developed by Archimedes, Riemann, and Lebesgue to measure shapes could be used to measure the unpredictable future. The chaotic laws of uncertainty were proven to be mathematically identical to the weight of a physical stone. By surrendering the need for physical space, Kolmogorov preserved the art of measuring for the quantum era.
