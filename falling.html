A rhythm reveals a law

In 1604, a man in Padua could not measure a second. So he used music. He rolled a ball down a plank, tied catgut frets along its edge, and adjusted them by ear until the clicks became a metronome. Then he measured where the frets had ended up. He had just heard the shape of gravity.

Galileo's Plank

He Used Music to Measure Time

Clocks in 1604 were too coarse. Minutes, if you were lucky. A second was a philosophical idea, not a measurement. And yet Galileo wanted to describe how things fall — which happens in less than a second, and during that second the thing changes quickly, in ways the eye cannot catch.

So he used music.

Experiment is the sole judge of scientific truth. — Richard Feynman, Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 1

In his workshop in Padua he built an inclined plank of seasoned wood, twelve cubits long, and carved along its length a straight channel, polished with parchment. Down this channel he rolled a bronze ball. Along the edge he tied small movable frets — thin strings of catgut, raised just enough that the rolling ball would strike them and click. Then he slid the frets up and down until, by his trained musical ear — and Galileo was raised by a father who was a composer — the clicks arrived at the beat of a simple song. Evenly spaced in time. A metronome of his own making.

Then he put down the ball and went to measure where the frets had ended up.

They were not evenly spaced. They were spaced in the ratio 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 — the odd numbers. That is the signature of t². Galileo did not know calculus. No one did. But his ear had just heard the shape of gravity.

The Water Clock

Weighing Time

But how did Galileo know the clicks were even? His ear told him — but how did he measure it, to write down numbers?

He used a water clock. A bucket with a small hole in the bottom, positioned above a collecting vessel on a scale. Open the hole at the start; close it at the end; weigh the water that came out. The mass of the outflow is proportional to the elapsed time.

He wrote that his measurements agreed "to one-tenth of a pulse beat." His pulse, he assumed, was also a reliable clock. In Galileo's hands, two ancient and imperfect instruments — a bucket and a wrist — became the first true chronometer of physics.

Three Curves, One Motion

A Parabola and Its Hidden Slopes

Here is what Galileo's odd-number law becomes, written in modern symbols:

x(t) = ½ · a · t²

A parabola. But there are two more curves hiding inside this one, and they are the ones Newton would isolate a generation later. The slope of x(t) at any instant is the velocity v(t) — a straight line rising from zero. The slope of v(t) is the acceleration a — a flat horizontal line.

Three curves, each the derivative of the one above. Galileo did not have this language. But you can watch the derivative happen, in real time, by eye.

Every Thrown Stone

The Arc Is a Parabola

In 1638, Galileo — old and blind and under house arrest for the crime of describing the solar system — dictated his last book to his son. In it he proved, for the first time, that every thrown stone traces a parabola. This connected cannonballs to the mathematics of the ancient Greeks — and it was a hint Newton would eventually complete: the parabola of a stone and the ellipse of a planet are the same conic section, cut at different angles through the same cone.

Galileo's argument was deceptively simple. A thrown stone has two motions happening at the same time, and they do not interfere with each other. Horizontally, it sails at constant velocity — nothing pushes it sideways. Vertically, it falls with constant acceleration — the rolling ball again, now invisible.

Add the two, and you get a parabola.

Fire the stone. Watch it split into two shadows.

The bright stone is real. The gold ghost on the floor has only the horizontal motion — sailing at constant speed, ignorant of gravity. The sky ghost on the left wall has only the vertical — falling from the peak as if released at rest. The real stone is always at the intersection of its two shadows.

The Tower at Pisa

Aristotle Was Wrong

The legend is that young Galileo climbed the Leaning Tower, dropped a cannonball and a musket ball from the top, and they struck the ground together — refuting Aristotle, who had taught for 1,900 years that heavier things fall faster. The legend is almost certainly apocryphal. Galileo never wrote of it. His student Viviani added the story to a biography sixty years later, after everyone who could contradict him was dead.

But the experiment is real, and you can try it in your hand. In vacuum, heavy and light fall together because gravity is exactly proportional to mass, so the mass cancels out. The Earth accelerates everything it touches at the same rate. The only thing that breaks this is air.

In 1971, Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the Moon, where there is no air, to settle it in front of a television camera. They landed together.

The Plumb Bob on the Ship

The Germ of Relativity

Finally: Galileo's most radical experiment, which he never actually performed, but which changed what it means to be at rest.

A ship sails at constant speed over calm water. You climb to the top of the mast and drop a plumb bob. Where does it land?

Aristotle would say: behind the mast. The ship moves while the bob falls; the bob has no sideways push, so the mast leaves it behind.

Galileo answered: at the base of the mast. Exactly at the base, every time, no matter how fast the ship is moving — as long as it is not accelerating. Because the bob already had the ship's horizontal velocity before you let go. Releasing it doesn't remove that velocity. So the bob and the mast drift sideways together, and the bob falls straight down relative to the ship.

The consequence is shattering. If you are inside a sealed cabin in that ship, you cannot tell whether the ship is moving or at rest, by any mechanical experiment. Drop things, throw things, watch water drip — everything behaves identically. This is Galilean relativity. Uniform motion is undetectable from inside. The Earth hurtles through space at 30 kilometers per second and we cannot feel it — because we are in the cabin, and the windows are fogged.

Three hundred years later, Einstein would push this principle one step further — asking what happens if even light obeys it — and out of that small extension fell all of modern physics.

Drop from the mast. Then switch frames.

6 m/s

Toggle the frame. From the shore, the bob traces a parabola — it inherited the ship's horizontal velocity. From the deck, it falls straight down along the mast. Both observers are right. This is the germ of relativity, 1632.

He was sentenced to house arrest for what he heard. Blind, dictating to his son from the villa at Arcetri, he published the law of falling bodies in a book smuggled to Holland. The Inquisition forbade him to teach. His plank stayed in the workshop, the frets still tied at positions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9.
