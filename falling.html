Mellow Math · Classical Mechanics · Ch. 2

The Shape of Falling

떨어짐의 모양
After Feynman, Lectures on Physics I.8 — "Motion"

Galileo could not measure short time intervals. Clocks in 1604 were too coarse: minutes, if you were lucky. A second was a philosophical idea, not a measurement. And yet he wanted to describe how things fall — which happens in less than a second, and during that second the thing changes, quickly, in ways the eye cannot catch.

So he used music.

In his workshop in Padua he built an inclined plank of seasoned wood, twelve cubits long, and carved along its length a straight channel, smooth as he could make it, polished with parchment. Down this channel he rolled a bronze ball. Along the edge of the plank he tied small movable frets — thin strings of catgut, raised just enough that the rolling ball would strike them and make a small audible click. Then he slid the frets up and down until, by his trained musical ear — and Galileo had been raised by a father who was a composer — the clicks arrived at the beat of a simple song. Evenly spaced in time. A metronome of his own making.

Then he put down the ball and went to measure where the frets had ended up.

They were not evenly spaced. They were spaced in the ratio 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 — the odd numbers.

That is the signature of t². The cumulative distance a ball rolls in equal time intervals is 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. The differences between those are the odd numbers. Galileo did not know calculus; no one did. But his ear had just discovered the shape of gravity.

"I took a piece of wooden moulding, about 12 cubits long, half a cubit wide, and three finger-breadths thick; on its edge was cut a channel a little more than one finger in breadth; having made this groove very straight, smooth, and polished, and having lined it with parchment, also as smooth and polished as possible, we rolled along it a hard, smooth, and very round bronze ball." — Galileo Galilei, Two New Sciences, 1638

This chapter is about what Galileo heard, and what it means. We'll use six interactive toys to hear it too, to measure time with water as he did, to watch parabolas emerge from sliders, to drop things from leaning towers, and to stand on a moving ship and understand, for the first time, why the Earth's rotation is undetectable from inside a room — which is the germ of relativity, three hundred years before Einstein.

I · The Musical Plank

Here is Galileo's apparatus. The inclined plank runs from upper left to lower right. Five catgut frets cross the channel, and you can drag each one up or down the slope. Press roll, and a bronze ball descends the channel and clicks as it crosses each fret. Your job: drag the frets until the clicks arrive evenly, as a steady rhythm. A metronome.

When the clicks are even, the fret positions will light up. Look at what they say.

II · How to Measure a Second

But how did Galileo know the clicks were even? His ear told him — but how did he measure it, to write down numbers? He used a water clock. Essentially a bucket with a small hole in the bottom, positioned above a collecting vessel on a scale. To time an event, open the hole at the start; close it at the end; weigh the water that came out.

The mass of the outflow is proportional to the elapsed time. Galileo could weigh to a grain. He wrote that his measurements agreed "to one-tenth of a pulse beat." His pulse, he assumed, was also a reliable clock.

III · The Parabola in Three Views

Here is what Galileo's odd-number law becomes, written in modern symbols:

x(t) = ½ · a · t²

A parabola. But there are two more curves hiding inside this one, and they are the ones Newton would isolate a generation later. The slope of x(t) at any instant is the velocity v(t), a straight line rising from zero. The slope of v(t) is the acceleration a, a flat horizontal line. Three curves, each the derivative of the one above. Watch them draw themselves as the ball rolls.

Module 3 · Position, Velocity, Acceleration

Three curves, one motion

The tangent line on the position curve has slope equal to the current velocity. The slope of the velocity line is the acceleration — constant, because the plank's incline is fixed. This is the derivative, discovered by eye.
IV · Every Thrown Stone

In 1638 Galileo, old and blind and under house arrest for the crime of describing the solar system, dictated his last book to his son. In it he proved, for the first time, that every thrown stone traces a parabola. This connected cannonballs to the mathematics of Apollonius — and it was a hint Newton would eventually complete: the parabola of a stone and the ellipse of a planet are the same conic section, cut at different angles through the same cone.

Galileo's argument was simple. A thrown stone has two motions happening at the same time, and they do not interfere with each other. Horizontally, it sails at constant velocity — nothing is pushing it sideways. Vertically, it falls with constant acceleration — exactly as in Module 3. Add the two, and you get a parabola. Let's decompose it.

Module 4 · The Decomposed Arc

Two motions at once

The bright ball is the real stone. The gold ghost on the floor has only the horizontal motion — moving at constant speed, ignorant of gravity. The cyan ghost on the left wall has only the vertical motion — falling straight down as if released from rest at the peak. The real stone is always at the intersection of its two shadows.
V · The Tower at Pisa

The legend is that young Galileo climbed the Leaning Tower of Pisa, dropped a cannonball and a musket ball simultaneously from the top, and they struck the ground at the same instant — refuting Aristotle, who had taught for 1,900 years that heavier objects fall faster. The legend is almost certainly apocryphal. Galileo never wrote of it. His student Vincenzo Viviani added the story to a biography sixty years later, after everyone who could contradict him was dead.

But the experiment is real, and you can try it in your hand. The reason heavy and light objects fall almost together is that the only force acting on them (in vacuum) is gravity, and gravity happens to be proportional to mass — so the mass cancels out. The acceleration is the same for all objects: the Earth accelerates everything it pulls on at exactly g.

The only thing that breaks this is air. In 1971, Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the Moon, where there is no air, to settle it finally in front of a television camera. They landed together.

VI · The Plumb Bob on the Ship

Finally: Galileo's most radical experiment, which he never actually performed, but which changed what it means to "be at rest."

A ship sails westward at constant speed over calm water. You climb to the top of the mast and drop a plumb bob. Where does it land?

Aristotle would say: behind the mast. The ship moves while the bob falls; the bob has no sideways push, so the mast leaves it behind.

Galileo answered: at the base of the mast. Exactly at the base, every time, no matter how fast the ship is moving — as long as it is not accelerating. Because the bob already had the ship's horizontal velocity before you let go. Releasing it doesn't remove that velocity. So the bob and the mast drift sideways together, and the bob falls straight down relative to the ship.

The consequence is shattering. If you are inside a sealed cabin in that ship, you cannot tell whether the ship is moving or at rest, by any mechanical experiment. Drop things, throw things, watch water drip — everything behaves identically. This is Galilean relativity. Uniform motion is undetectable from inside. The Earth hurtles through space at 30 kilometers per second and we cannot feel it — because we are in the cabin, and the windows are fogged.

Toggle the frame. From the shore, the bob traces a parabola — it has horizontal velocity inherited from the ship. From the deck, it falls straight down along the mast. Both observers are right. This is the germ of relativity, 1632.

Galileo published these ideas in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems in 1632, at the age of 68. The Inquisition summoned him to Rome, tried him for heresy, and sentenced him to house arrest for the remainder of his life. He was forbidden to publish or teach.

He went back to his villa at Arcetri, outside Florence. There, in the silence imposed on him, he dictated his final book — Two New Sciences — to his son Vincenzio and a devoted student, Evangelista Torricelli, the young man who would soon invent the barometer and work out the flow of the water clocks that had timed the falling balls. The book was smuggled to Holland and printed in Leiden in 1638. By then Galileo was blind. He did not live to see a printed copy.

But his plank was still in the workshop. The frets were still tied along its edge, at positions spaced 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. A rhythm, silent now, waiting to be heard again.
