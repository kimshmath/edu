When you add two waves, why does the result still repeat periodically?

The magic of two close tones · sum → product

Beats
a sum turns into a product

Add two nearly equal tones and why does the sound swell slowly?

$$ \sin A+\sin B = 2\,\cos\tfrac{A-B}{2}\,\sin\tfrac{A+B}{2} $$
The sum of two sines becomes a product of a cosine and a sine — a single sum-to-product identity explains beats.

Average 440 Hz  ·  beats 4.0 Hz

a fast carrier breathing inside a slow envelope
$$ \sin 2\pi f_1 t+\sin 2\pi f_2 t \;=\; 2\,\cos\!\big(2\pi\tfrac{f_1-f_2}{2}\,t\big)\,\sin\!\big(2\pi\tfrac{f_1+f_2}{2}\,t\big) $$
Slow envelope = half-the-difference frequency · fast carrier = average frequency · what you hear is |envelope|, so beats = |f₁−f₂| per second

The identity of timbre

Timbre is, in the end,
what?

Same pitch, same loudness — so what tells the instruments apart? The ratios of the harmonic amplitudes \(A_1,A_2,A_3,\dots\).

  • Bright soundstrong high harmonics (brass · sawtooth)
  • Round soundmostly low harmonics (flute · sine)
  • Hollow soundodd harmonics dominant (clarinet · square wave)
One step further — real instruments

In a real instrument the harmonic distribution changes over time. Attack, decay, and even faint noise are part of “that instrument.” The static spectrum is a good first approximation — the changes come in the Part 5 spectrogram.

PART FOUR

Fourier Series

Can we listen to a sound and read off its recipe in reverse?

Joseph Fourier · 1807

Can we really write “every” periodic function
as a sum of sines?

$$ f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big[a_n\cos\tfrac{2\pi n t}{T} + b_n\sin\tfrac{2\pi n t}{T}\Big] $$
T period (one cycle of the fundamental) · n-th term = the n× frequency component · aₙ, bₙ = how much of that component to mix in (the very values we set with the sliders!)

Discovered while solving heat conduction — a shocking claim in its day.

A wave with two faces

Is a sum of cos and sin
a single sine?

At the same frequency, \(a_n\cos + b_n\sin\) → a single sine with an amplitude and a phase.

$$ a_n\cos\theta + b_n\sin\theta = A_n\sin(\theta+\varphi_n) $$
$$A_n=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}\qquad \tan\varphi_n=\tfrac{a_n}{b_n}$$
One musical fact

Timbre is almost entirely set by the harmonic magnitudes Aₙ. Scramble the phases \(\varphi_n\) and the waveform changes, yet the ear hears the same sound (Ohm's phase law). That's why the “bars” alone can describe timbre.

Reading the recipe in reverse · a resonance detector

How do we pull out a hidden frequency?

How do we know whether a given frequency is in the sound? — multiply and average. If it matches, it piles up large; if not, it cancels to 0.

×1.0
Signal = fundamental + 2nd & 3rd harmonics. Try sweeping the test frequency.
signal × test → product (area = detection value)
detection vs test frequency · peaks only at harmonics
The formula for what we just did

Integration = an averaging machine

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_{0}^{T} f(t)\,\cos\tfrac{2\pi n t}{T}\,dt $$
Take the signal, multiply by the n× frequency cosine, and average (integrate) over one period = the magnitude of that component. Same for bₙ with the sine.
$$ \int_0^T \!\sin\tfrac{2\pi m t}{T}\,\sin\tfrac{2\pi n t}{T}\,dt = 0 \;\;(m\neq n) $$
Orthogonality — different harmonics multiply and average to 0, so each component is extracted “cleanly,” without interference.

Synthesis (building) ↔ analysis (reading) — the same sine-wave language, a perfect symmetry.

PART FIVE

Fourier Transform

How do we analyze a sound that never repeats forever?

Blueprint of the opening-screen machine · coefficients as numbers

Circle radius = Fourier coefficient

$$ a_n=\tfrac{2}{N}\!\sum_{k} f_k\cos\tfrac{2\pi nk}{N},\ \ b_n=\tfrac{2}{N}\!\sum_{k} f_k\sin\tfrac{2\pi nk}{N} $$ $$ A_n=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2},\qquad \varphi_n=\operatorname{atan2}(a_n,b_n) $$

One harmonic \(n\) = a circle of radius Aₙ that makes n turns per period, starting at angle φₙ. Adding these circles tip-to-tail is the rotating machine on the opening screen.

From series to transform

Stretch the period to infinity —
what happens?

Grow the period \(T\) without bound? — the spacing \(1/T\to 0\), the bars become a continuous spectrum, and the sum \(\sum\) becomes an integral \(\int\).

One elegant line

Can we bundle sine and cosine into one?

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\,\sin\theta $$
$$ \hat f(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt $$
The Fourier transform — multiply the signal f(t) by the “probe spinning at frequency ω,” \(e^{-i\omega t}\), and average. The magnitude \(|\hat f(\omega)|\) = how much of that frequency is present = the spectrum.

The “multiply and average” of slide 17 — for every frequency at once.

An unavoidable trade-off

Can we know time and frequency
at the same time?

$$ \Delta t \cdot \Delta f \;\gtrsim\; \frac{1}{4\pi} $$
Make the analysis window short → time is sharp, frequency blurs. Long → frequency is sharp, time blurs.
The same math in two worlds

This trade-off is the same formula as the uncertainty principle in quantum mechanics. It's also why the pitch of a very short percussive hit is ambiguous.

Your ear · a living spectrum analyzer

Does the ear itself
perform a Fourier transform?

How does the ear turn frequency into position? — a traveling wave on the basilar membrane peaks at a different place for each frequency, and the hair cells there fire.

The entrance is narrow and stiff → high notes; the far end is wide and soft → low notes. The whole piano range in just 3.5 cm.

440 Hz

The glowing hairs = cells firing right now. Each cell responds to only a specific frequency → a hardware Fourier transform.

the cochlea unrolled · traveling wave and hair cells
Spectrum = neural signal · place code
several harmonics = several peaks on the basilar membrane

Is the spectrum we computed
also inside the ear?

A harmonic-rich sound resonates at several positions at once — this excitation pattern is the spectrum. The map is preserved up to the auditory cortex (a place code). Pitch = which place, timbre = the pattern of peaks.

Strictly speaking, it isn't a pure Fourier analyzer — a finite time window · logarithmic-spacing resolution · nonlinear amplification by the outer hair cells. It's actually closer to the wavelet we just saw!
