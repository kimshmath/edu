01 — The Sentence

One Line, All of Physics

In the autumn of 1961, Richard Feynman stood in front of a freshman class at Caltech and asked a strange question. If all scientific knowledge were destroyed in some cataclysm, and only a single sentence could be passed down — which sentence would carry the most information in the fewest words?

He answered himself immediately.

All things are made of atoms — little particles that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another. — R. P. Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 1

In that single sentence, he said, an enormous amount of information about the world is contained — if just a little imagination and thinking are applied. What follows are six attempts to spend those two things carefully. Six exhibits. Each is Feynman's sentence, made visible.

Exhibit I — Temperature

Heat Is Speed

When you wrap your hands around a warm cup of tea, your fingers are not sensing some invisible fluid seeping into your skin. They are sensing a drumbeat. Billions of fast water molecules strike the inside of the ceramic; the ceramic's own atoms jiggle harder in reply; that jiggling passes, collision by collision, into the nerves beneath your palms. What you call warmth is a statistic — the average speed of a crowd you cannot see.

Exhibit II — The Box

Two Hundred Impacts

In 1860, a young Scottish physicist named James Clerk Maxwell asked a question that sounds impossible to answer. If you fill a box with atoms and let them collide endlessly, what does the distribution of their speeds look like? The speed of any single atom is chaotic — but the shape of the crowd, Maxwell suggested, can be computed from pure statistics.

The curve he derived is slightly asymmetric, with a long tail toward high speeds. It was the first great victory of statistical thinking in physics. Below, that curve assembles itself in real time from two hundred bouncing disks. The amber disk carries a trail — its wandering path is Brownian motion, the same jittering Robert Brown watched under his microscope in 1827 without being able to explain.

Exhibit III — Brownian Motion

What Robert Brown Saw

On a June afternoon in 1827, the Scottish botanist Robert Brown placed a drop of water containing pollen under his microscope and watched the tiny particles inside refuse to sit still. They jerked. They drifted. They traced irregular paths with no apparent cause. Brown tested dead wood, ground glass, even dust from a fragment of the Great Sphinx — everything jittered. He published his observations and moved on. Nobody could explain them for seventy-eight years.

In 1905, a twenty-six-year-old patent clerk in Bern worked it out with a pencil. If atoms exist, Einstein argued, a grain of pollen suspended in water must be kicked by billions of invisible impacts per second. Statistically, a slight net push will survive. He derived the signature: the mean squared distance the grain wanders grows linearly with time — not as time squared, the way a falling object's does, but simply as time. Below, that prediction proves itself.

Exhibit IV — Diffusion

Why Smells Cross Rooms

Open a bottle of perfume at one end of a quiet room and wait. No wind, no draft — and yet, within minutes, the scent arrives at your nose. Each fragrance molecule, alone, performs a drunken walk: collide, tumble, collide again. The path of any single one is unpredictable. But the crowd of them spreads outward, reliably, in a cloud whose width grows as the square root of time.

Exhibit V — Pressure

What Pushes on the Walls

A tire holds up a car through nothing but percussion. Billions of air molecules strike the rubber every second, each rebound delivering a tiny push. Together they lift a ton of steel. In 1662 Robert Boyle observed that halving a gas's volume exactly doubles its pressure — and for 250 years, the law bore his name without anyone knowing why it was true.

From the atoms' side, the answer is trivial. Squeeze the box; each molecule has to hit the walls twice as often. The drumbeat quickens, and pressure rises in lockstep. Drag the green piston below.

Exhibit VI — The Anomaly

Why Ice Floats

Nearly every substance gets denser when it freezes. Molten lead, paraffin wax, frozen mercury — all sink in their own liquids. Water refuses. Because its molecules are polar — a slightly negative oxygen, two slightly positive hydrogens — they pull on each other not only by ordinary attraction but by directional hydrogen bonds. When water freezes, it arranges itself to maximize those bonds, and the arrangement is hexagonal: open, roomy, about nine percent less dense than the liquid crowd it came from.

The consequence is not small. A lake in winter freezes at the surface first, and the ice floats. It forms an insulating lid. Fish survive. Frogs hibernate in the mud beneath. Complex ecosystems at temperate latitudes owe their winters to this single geometric quirk of a three-atom molecule.

Epilogue

Nothing Is Still

The sentence Feynman chose, if one had to choose only one, is in the end a sentence about restlessness. There is no still thing. The walls around you are a lattice trembling in place; the air above them is a crowd in perpetual wander; the warmth of your own body is a measured speed.

Feynman returned to this thought often. A few pages later in the same chapter, reaching for the broadest possible statement, he wrote —

Everything that living things do can be understood in terms of the jigglings and wigglings of atoms. — R. P. Feynman, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, Ch. 1

What makes this picture beautiful is not its utility, though it is useful. What makes it beautiful is its generosity. The same statistical grammar that governs the drift of pollen grains in water governs the diffusion of perfume across a room, the pressure that holds up a tire, the hexagonal lattice that lets fish survive beneath a frozen lake. One picture — atoms, motion, collision — and an entire world lights up from inside.

You will put this page down and walk into a kitchen. You will touch a counter, feel its coolness, smell something on the stove, fill a glass from the tap. None of these sensations will be different from what they were before. But for the rest of your life, you will know that every one of them is this.

To see a World in a Grain of Sand
And a Heaven in a Wild Flower,
Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand
And Eternity in an hour.
— William Blake, Auguries of Innocence, c. 1803
